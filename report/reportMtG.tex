\begin{abstract}
  Quantum Computing architectures based on Quantum Annealing (QA) offer a promising framework to solve NP-hard optimization problems efficiently, yet their effectiveness is constrained by hardware topology. 
  Solving an Optimization Problem $P$ by QA means to represent $P$ as a graph $G_P$ and to map it on the graph $G_Q$ which physically implements a QA-based quantum processing unit (QPU).
  Minor Embedding (ME) is the heuristic for the map between $G_P$ and $G_Q$. 
  ME associates every node of $G_P$ -- the logical variables of $P$ -- to a chain of adjacent nodes in $G_Q$ -- chains of physical qubits --, preserving the connectivity of $G_P$, coherently with the connectivity of $G_Q$.
  ME introduces both overhead and error sensitivity, the connectivity of $G_Q$ resulting in a potential bottleneck towards performance.

  As for $G_P$ we focus on cliques representing dense optimization problems.
  Concerning $G_Q$, we consider Zephyr graphs -- current D-Wave's choice for QPUs --, and Havel-Hakimi graphs, interesting because they allow to study the role of degree distribution on embeddability without geometric constraints.
  Then, we propose measures, depending on a wide set of parameters to assess how ME performs on the chosen $G_P$ and $G_Q$.

  Our findings promote the idea that Havel-Hakimi-based QPU topology would allow to get a smoother scaling when parameters known as strongly related to QPU computational performance and capacity to find optimal answers.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

In the current landscape of quantum computing, two principal paradigms can be identified.
The former is \emph{digital quantum computing}, which relies on quantum gates.
The latter is \emph{analog quantum computing}, which is typically realized through adiabatic programming.
Adiabatic Quantum Computing (AQC)~\cite{AQC} is theoretically universal; however, no practical implementation has been achieved so far.
\emph{Quantum annealing}~\cite{QA} is a specialized, non-universal instance of AQC that is nonetheless effective for addressing NP-hard optimization problems.

The quantum annealing workflow can be summarized in three key steps:
\begin{enumerate*}
  \item Express the problem in Quadratic Unconstrained Binary Optimization (QUBO) form~\cite{lucas};
  \item Embed the problem graph into the hardware graph of the quantum processing unit (QPU)~\cite{ME, MEdwave, MENP, localembedding};
  \item Execute the quantum annealing process to obtain solutions.
\end{enumerate*}

The primary objective of this work is to quantitatively assess how different QPU topologies $G_Q$ influence the embeddability of dense problem graphs $G_P$, highlighting structural factors that affect scalability and stability. 
We focus on cliques as representative cases of dense $G_P$ and compare Zephyr graphs---current D-Wave's choice for $G_Q$---with alternative Havel-Hakimi constructions.
Our analysis evaluates the efficiency of the minor embedding procedure in producing the embedded graph $G_E$, depending on a wide set of parameters related to $G_Q$.
Our findings promote the idea that Havel–Hakimi–based QPU topologies could enable smoother scaling with respect to parameters known to influence computational performance and the capacity to reach optimal solutions.

\paragraph{Minor embedding}
Once the problem is expressed in QUBO form, it must be transformed to match the fixed connectivity of the QPU topology.
This step, known as \emph{minor embedding}, is crucial because hardware graphs are not fully connected.
Conceptually, a single logical variable may be mapped to a chain of physical qubits, as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:embedding}.
This allows the problem graph to be embedded within the QPU graph, with longer chains of physical qubits increasing susceptibility to noise.
We remark that the study of minor embedding has implications beyond quantum computing itself, as it shares structural analogies with placement and routing problems in VLSI design, where logical circuits must also be efficiently mapped onto physically constrained architectures.

\paragraph{Preliminary work}
In earlier work~\cite{mscthesis}, we showed that the D-Wave Advantage QPU, based on the Pegasus topology~\cite{Pegasus}, can embed problems of up to 100 binary variables, despite having more than 5000 physical qubits.
The most recent generation of D-Wave annealers adopts the Zephyr topology, with about 4000 qubits but higher connectivity that can successfully embed cliques up to 153 nodes.
The primary objective of this work is to quantitatively assess how different QPU topologies influence the embeddability of dense problem graphs, highlighting structural factors that affect scalability and stability.

While we acknowledge that practical QPU design must balance theoretical expressivity with physical stability, we argue that systematic studies of topological properties can provide useful guidance for hardware development.
Such investigations may guide the exploration of trade-offs between stability and expressivity.

\section{QPU Topology}

The connectivity of a QPU, represented by its graph $G_Q$, can be analyzed at both coarse-grained and fine-grained levels. 

At a coarse level, $G_Q$ can be described by the number of nodes and edges, from which one can derive the average node degree. 
However, this information alone is not sufficient to predict embedding performance: the detailed connectivity pattern of $G_Q$—its regularity, modularity, and degree distribution—strongly influences how a problem graph $G_P$ can be mapped onto it to form the embedded graph $G_E$.

\subsection{Zephyr Graph}\label{sec:zephyr}

The Zephyr graph~\cite{QPU} is parameterized by:
\begin{enumerate*}
  \item the grid size $m$,
  \item and the tile size $t$.
\end{enumerate*}

The graph consists of an $m \times m$ grid of unit blocks.
Each block resembles a cross-shaped unit, with $t$ nodes extending in each direction from the center.

In our study, we explored combinations of $m$ and $t$ in the range $[2,7] \times [1,25]$, yielding 150 graphs, with sizes ranging from 40 nodes and 114 edges to 10,500 nodes and 508,750 edges.

\subsection{Havel-Hakimi Graph}\label{sec:hh}

The Havel-Hakimi graphs are generated by specifying:
\begin{enumerate*}
  \item the uniform degree $deg$ of each node,
  \item and the total number of nodes $num\_qubits$.
\end{enumerate*}

The Havel-Hakimi algorithm~\cite{hh} iteratively constructs a graph by connecting the node of the highest remaining degree to others of the highest degree, resorting the residual sequence, and repeating until completion.
This procedure yields graphs that are less structured than Zephyr ones but exhibit controlled degree distributions.

In our experiments, we generated regular Havel-Hakimi graphs, where each node has the same degree.
We considered $deg \in \{5+25k \mid k=0,\dots,4\}$ and $num\_qubits \in \{50+350m \mid m=0,\dots,29\}$, resulting in 150 graphs, ranging from 50 nodes and 125 edges to 10,200 nodes and 535,500 edges.

\paragraph{Note on QPU connectivity constraints} Across all generated QPU topologies, we constrained the maximum node degree to values close to 100.
This upper bound is motivated by physical and technological considerations: the connectivity of current quantum annealers grows slowly due to fabrication and control limitations.
For instance, the Zephyr architecture features a maximum degree of 20, which already represents an improvement over previous generations.
Therefore, degrees beyond 100 would correspond to purely theoretical configurations that are unlikely to be realized in the near future.
By adopting this limit, we ensure that our analysis remains both physically grounded and forward-looking, capturing trends relevant to forthcoming QPU generations while avoiding unrealistic regimes.

\section{Methodology}

To build our dataset~\cite{repo}, we generated Zephyr graphs representing $G_Q$ using \texttt{zephyr\_graph} from the D-Wave library and Havel–Hakimi graphs using \texttt{havel\_hakimi\_graph} from \texttt{networkx}. 
For each instance of $G_Q$, we searched for the largest clique $G_P = K_n$ that could be successfully embedded, using the \texttt{find\_embedding} function from \texttt{minorminer}. 
The embedding process produces an embedded graph $G_E$, where each node of $G_P$ is associated with a chain of adjacent qubits in $G_Q$, preserving the logical connectivity.

Cliques serve as a natural benchmark: once a problem is translated into QUBO form, it typically yields highly connected graphs.
In our analysis, we focused on generic cliques without associating them with specific problems.

From this experimental procedure, we collected the following data:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Graph properties:
        \begin{enumerate*}
          \item graph type,
          \item construction parameters,
          \item number of nodes,
          \item number of edges,
          \item average node degree;
        \end{enumerate*}
  \item The dimension of the largest embeddable clique;
  \item Qualitative metrics describing the embedding structure, specifically:
        \begin{enumerate*}
          \item mean,
          \item median,
          \item mode
        \end{enumerate*} of the qubit chain lengths produced by the minor embedding;
\end{itemize}

To enable comparison between different topological families of $G_Q$, both the average degree and the size of the largest embeddable clique were normalized by the number of qubits in $G_Q$. 
This normalization allows all configurations to be represented in a common parameter space, facilitating the identification of structural correlations between $G_Q$ and the resulting embedding capacity of $G_E$.

Figure~\ref{fig:zephyr_vs_hh} summarizes the results of our embedding experiments. 
The horizontal axis represents the normalized average degree of the QPU topology $G_Q$, while the vertical axis shows the normalized size of the largest clique $G_P$ that the minor embedding algorithm can successfully map onto it, producing $G_E$. 
Distinct scaling behaviors emerge for Zephyr and Havel–Hakimi graphs, highlighting how the structural properties of $G_Q$ influence embedding capacity.

The blue dots, corresponding to Havel-Hakimi configurations, exhibit an approximately linear relationship: as the degree of the QPU increases, the size of the embeddable clique grows proportionally.
This trend is intuitively expected, since higher connectivity directly improves the embedding capacity.

In contrast, the red dots, representing Zephyr configurations, display a sublinear relationship.
Although Zephyr topologies demonstrate higher expressivity at lower degrees, their embedding capacity grows more slowly as connectivity increases.

Overall, embeddings on Havel–Hakimi graphs tend to produce shorter qubit chains, as we can see in Figure~\ref{fig:chains}, and exhibit smoother scaling with degree, suggesting more compact and stable $G_E$ structures. 
Zephyr topologies, in contrast, perform better at lower degrees—corresponding to the practical connectivity limits of existing QPUs—making them well suited to current hardware implementations.

\section{Future work}

Future work will extend this comparative analysis to a broader set of synthetic and physically inspired QPU topologies $G_Q$. 
We also plan to investigate correlations between structural indicators of $G_Q$ and practical embedding quality metrics, such as chain stability and noise susceptibility in $G_E$. 
As a further direction, we intend to explore new candidate topologies derived from graph-theoretical principles, focusing on structures that could minimize the length of qubit chains while maintaining feasible connectivity and manufacturability. 
These studies aim to support the co-design of hardware architectures and embedding heuristics, promoting QPU topologies that balance physical realizability, connectivity, and embedding efficiency.
